Where coffee begins

Coffee only grows well within a narrow band around the equator, nicknamed the "coffee belt" — and the story of how it got there starts in an Ethiopian forest.

According to the most enduring legend, a goat herder named Kaldi noticed his herd growing unusually energetic after eating berries from a certain shrub. Word of the plant reached monasteries, and by the 15th century, coffee cultivation and trade had spread across the Arabian Peninsula, moving through Sufi communities and the coffeehouses of Mecca and Cairo.

European traders carried it further from the 17th century onward, and colonial plantations eventually established the crop across Latin America, the Caribbean, and Southeast Asia. Today the coffee belt runs roughly between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn — Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia, Ethiopia, and Indonesia are consistently among the largest producers.

The plant itself is fussy. It wants volcanic or mineral-rich soil, consistent rainfall, moderate temperatures, and — for the more delicate arabica variety — high altitude, often above 1,000 metres. Small changes in elevation, shade, and rainfall are part of why coffee from neighbouring farms can taste noticeably different.

From cherry to cup: the six steps

Every bag of coffee, regardless of where it was grown, passes through the same broad sequence before it's ready to brew.

01

Harvest

Ripe cherries are picked either by hand — selectively, over several passes — or by machine, which strips branches all at once regardless of ripeness. Hand-picking is slower but allows only the ripest cherries to be selected.

02

Processing

The fruit around the seed has to come off. "Washed" coffee is pulped and fermented before drying, giving a cleaner, brighter cup. "Natural" coffee is dried with the whole cherry intact, producing a fruitier, heavier body. "Honey" processing falls in between.

03

Drying

Beans are spread on raised beds or patios and turned regularly for one to three weeks, until their internal moisture drops to roughly 10–12%, low enough for safe storage and transport.

04

Milling and grading

The dried parchment layer is removed, and beans are sorted by size, density, and colour. Defective beans are pulled out at this stage, and the remainder is graded and bagged as green coffee.

05

Roasting

Green beans are heated to well over 200°C, triggering the Maillard reaction and caramelisation that create coffee's familiar aroma and colour. "First crack" and "second crack" — audible pops as the beans expand — mark key points in the roast.

06

Grinding and brewing

Grind size is matched to the brewing method — coarse for a French press, fine for espresso — and hot or cold water is used to dissolve the soluble compounds that give coffee its flavour.

What roast level actually changes

Roasting doesn't just make beans darker — it trades the farm's original character for flavours created by heat itself.

Light roast

Roasted only until shortly after first crack. The surface stays dry, with no visible oil. Origin flavours — floral, fruity, citric — stay most prominent, along with brighter acidity.

Medium roast

Taken further into or just past first crack, balancing the bean's own character with new caramelised sweetness. This is the most common roast level for everyday drip and pour-over coffee.

Dark roast

Pushed toward or past second crack, when oils migrate to the surface, giving beans their characteristic sheen. Roast flavour — smoky, bittersweet, chocolatey — dominates over the original growing region's character.

Four ways to turn grounds into coffee

Every method changes how much time water spends with the grounds, and how much pressure is involved — and that's most of what defines the cup's body and clarity.

Manual, filtered

Pour-over

Water is poured in slow, controlled circles over grounds sitting in a paper or metal filter. The paper traps most of the coffee's natural oils, giving a clean, tea-like cup that shows off subtle, delicate flavours clearly.

Manual, immersion

French press

Coarse grounds steep fully submerged in hot water for about four minutes, then a metal mesh plunger separates the liquid from the grounds. Because there's no paper filter, more oils and fine sediment pass through, giving a heavier, fuller-bodied cup.

Pressurised

Espresso

Near-boiling water is forced through finely-ground, tightly-packed coffee at high pressure in about 25–30 seconds. The result is a small, concentrated shot topped with crema — a light, reddish-brown foam — and it's the base for drinks like lattes and cappuccinos.

Cold, slow

Cold brew

Coarse grounds steep in room-temperature or cold water for 12 to 24 hours, rather than using heat to speed up extraction. The result is naturally sweeter and noticeably less acidic, usually made as a concentrate that's diluted before drinking.

How the world drinks it differently

Coffee took on a distinct identity everywhere it landed — the drink itself, and the ritual around it, vary enormously by region.

Ethiopia — the coffee ceremony

Green beans are roasted fresh in a pan in front of guests, ground by hand, and brewed in a clay pot called a jebena. Three rounds are traditionally served — abol, tona, and baraka — and the whole ritual, incense included, can take over an hour.

Italy — the standing espresso

Espresso is typically drunk quickly, standing at the bar counter, rather than lingered over at a table. Cappuccino is culturally treated as a morning drink, rarely ordered after a meal.

Turkey — unfiltered and simmered

Very finely ground coffee is simmered, unfiltered, in a small long-handled pot called a cezve. It's served with the grounds still settled at the bottom of the cup, and reading the leftover patterns is a well-known folk tradition.

Vietnam — robusta and condensed milk

Coffee is brewed slowly through a small metal drip filter called a phin, directly over sweetened condensed milk. Served hot or, very commonly, poured over ice.

The Nordic countries — the coffee break as ritual

Nordic countries consistently rank among the highest per-capita coffee consumers in the world. Light roasts are preferred, and a paused, unhurried coffee break with something to eat is treated as a genuine social occasion rather than a quick refuel.

A few things worth knowing

Some of the most-asked questions about coffee, answered plainly.

Arabica vs. robusta

Arabica accounts for roughly 60–70% of global production. It's grown at higher altitudes, is more sensitive to pests and climate, and produces a smoother cup with more nuanced flavour and comparatively less caffeine.

Robusta is hardier, grows well at lower altitudes, and contains close to double the caffeine of arabica. Its flavour is generally more bitter and earthy, which is why it's often used in espresso blends for extra body and crema, and in instant coffee.

ArabicaHigher altitude, more delicate flavour, lower caffeine, more vulnerable to disease.
RobustaLower altitude, stronger and more bitter, higher caffeine, more disease-resistant.

Caffeine and decaf

Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain, the same receptors that build up a feeling of tiredness over the day. Its effects are usually felt within 30 to 60 minutes, and its half-life in most healthy adults is roughly three to five hours.

Decaffeination happens before roasting, on green beans. The Swiss Water Process uses only water, temperature, and activated carbon filters, with no chemical solvents. Solvent-based methods use compounds like ethyl acetate, while the CO2 method uses pressurised carbon dioxide. Most methods remove around 97% of the original caffeine.
